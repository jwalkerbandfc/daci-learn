Foundations of 3D Modelling

Preparation for Blender. This resource introduces the core ideas behind every 3D model: how position is described in 3D space, what a mesh is made of, and which polygon types to use. Work through each section in order and complete the checks for learning as you go.

Learning objectives

By the end of this resource, you will be able to:

  1. Identify the X, Y and Z axes and describe how they define position in 3D space.
  2. Explain the difference between local and global coordinates.
  3. Identify vertices, edges and faces as the component parts of a mesh.
  4. Distinguish between tris, quads and n-gons.
  5. Justify why quads are the preferred polygon type in most modelling workflows.
Key terms glossary
Axis
A straight reference line used to measure position. 3D space has three axes: X, Y and Z.
Origin
The point where all axes meet, at coordinates (0, 0, 0). Objects in Blender also have their own origin point.
Coordinate
A number describing a position along one axis. A point in 3D needs three coordinates (X, Y, Z).
Global
Coordinates and axes measured from the world origin. Global axes are fixed and never rotate.
Local
Coordinates and axes that belong to an object. Local axes move and rotate with the object.
Vertex
A single point in 3D space defined by X, Y and Z coordinates. Plural: vertices.
Edge
A straight line connecting two vertices.
Face
A flat surface enclosed by three or more edges.
Mesh
A collection of vertices, edges and faces that together define the shape of a 3D object.
Polygon
A closed shape made of straight edges. In 3D modelling, "polygon" and "face" are often used to mean the same thing.
Tri
A face with three vertices (a triangle).
Quad
A face with four vertices (a quadrilateral).
N-gon
A face with five or more vertices.
Topology
The arrangement and flow of vertices, edges and faces across a mesh.
Edge loop
A connected line of edges that runs across a mesh through a series of quads, often returning to its start.
Subdivision
A process that splits each face into smaller faces, often smoothing the surface at the same time.
Wireframe
A display mode that shows only the edges of a mesh, without filled faces.
1

3D Space and the X, Y and Z Axes

From 2D to 3D

2D space is flat, like a sheet of paper or a sprite in a 2D game. Any point can be described with two numbers:

  • X horizontal position (left and right)
  • Y vertical position (up and down on the page)

3D space adds a third axis, Z, which adds depth. A point in 3D is written as (X, Y, Z).

In Blender, Z points up. X and Y describe position across the floor, and Z describes height.

A point in 2D

In 2D, a point needs two coordinates. In 3D, it needs a third coordinate for Z.

Positive and negative directions

Every axis has a positive and a negative direction, measured from the origin (0, 0, 0). In the viewport, the solid part of each axis line is positive and the dashed part is negative.

Global and local coordinates

  • Global (world) axes are fixed to the scene. They always point in the same directions, whatever an object is doing.
  • Local axes belong to an object. When the object rotates, its local axes rotate with it.

Try it: set the Z rotation to 45°, then press +X in Global and in Local orientation. Global moves the cube along the red grid line. Local moves it in the direction the cube is facing.

In Blender, choose the orientation from the Transform Orientation menu in the viewport header. Pressing an axis key twice while moving (for example G X X) switches to the local axis.

Z-up and Y-up: why it matters when exporting

Blender uses a Z-up coordinate system. Some game engines, including Unity, use a Y-up system. Unreal Engine is Z-up, but uses a different handedness.

When you export a model (for example as FBX), the exporter must convert between systems. If the axis settings are wrong, the model may appear rotated by 90°, lying on its back or facing the wrong way. You may also see an unexpected rotation value, such as -90° on X, on the imported object.

Always check the Forward and Up settings in Blender's export options, and test imports early in your engine.

Vertices, Edges and Faces

Every 3D model you build in Blender is a mesh: a collection of three types of component.

Vertex

A single point in 3D space defined by X, Y and Z coordinates.

Edge

A straight line connecting two vertices.

Face

A flat surface enclosed by three or more edges.

What to notice

  • Each vertex stores only a position: three coordinates.
  • Edges do not store a position of their own. They are defined by the two vertices they connect.
  • Faces are defined by their surrounding edges. Moving one vertex changes every edge and face attached to it.

Compare the counts

  • Plane: 4 vertices, 4 edges, 1 face.
  • Cube: 8 vertices, 12 edges, 6 faces.
  • Cylinder: the top and bottom are single faces with 8 vertices each. You will meet this type of face again in Section 3.

Blender's default cylinder has 32 sides, so its counts are much higher.

What to look for: on the left, the quads divide into an even grid and the surface curves smoothly. On the right, many long, thin faces meet at one central point (a pole). Look for pinching around that point and uneven shading rippling out from the centre. Use Top view and the wireframe overlay to compare.

Why quads are preferred

  • Predictable subdivision and smoothing. A quad always splits into four smaller quads, giving an even, smooth surface.
  • Clean edge loops and edge flow. Edge loops pass straight through quads, so you can select and edit whole rings of geometry in one click (Alt + click in Blender). Tris and n-gons stop a loop.
  • Better deformation when rigged and animated. Evenly spaced quads bend cleanly at joints such as elbows and knees. Tris and n-gons can crease or collapse.
  • Easier UV unwrapping. Quad grids flatten into regular, predictable UV layouts, which makes texturing simpler.

Are tris ever acceptable?

Yes. Game engines and graphics cards only draw triangles. Every quad and n-gon is converted to triangles at render time, so tris are acceptable in final game assets.

However, you should model in quads because it keeps the mesh easier to edit, subdivide and animate. Triangulate at the end of the process, or let the engine do it on import.

N-gons are also acceptable on flat surfaces that will not deform or be subdivided, but the engine may triangulate them in unexpected ways.
