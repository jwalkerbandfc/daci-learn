    Blender Materials and Shading

    Your models have clean topology and smooth shading. Now give them a surface: shiny metal, frosted glass, glazed ceramic, glowing screens and textured wood. Learn the Principled BSDF, the Shader Editor, textures and UV unwrapping, and see how EEVEE and Cycles render them.

    Start with the warm-up Jump to the material lab

    Learning outcomes

    By the end you will be able to:

    • switch between Solid, Material Preview and Rendered views, and explain what each is for;
    • choose between EEVEE and Cycles for a task, and explain how they differ;
    • create, reuse and link materials, and give different faces of one object different materials;
    • use the main Principled BSDF settings — Base Color, Metallic, Roughness, IOR, Transmission, Emission, Alpha, Coat, Sheen and Subsurface — to match real materials;
    • read and build simple node trees in the Shader Editor;
    • use procedural and image textures with the correct colour space, and add surface detail with bump, normal maps and displacement;
    • mark seams, unwrap a model and check the result with a UV test grid;
    • recognise and fix common material and texture problems.

    What is a material?

    A material describes what a surface is made of and how light behaves when it hits it: whether light bounces off sharply (polished metal), scatters (chalk), passes through (glass) or is given off (a screen). In Blender almost every material starts with one node, the Principled BSDF, which can make most real-world surfaces by changing a handful of settings.

    ◐Colour

    Base Color — the colour of the surface, or of a metal’s reflections.

    ✦Shine

    Roughness — from mirror-sharp (0) to completely matte (1).

    ⬢Metal or not

    Metallic — 0 for plastic, wood, paint and stone; 1 for metals.

    ◇Special effects

    Transmission, Emission, Coat, Sheen, Subsurface — glass, glow, varnish, fabric and skin.

    Good to know

    Materials need the right view

    Solid view shows a plain grey model. Press Z and choose Material Preview or Rendered to see your materials.

    Shader Editor shortcuts

    Shortcuts act on the editor under your mouse. Shift+A adds a mesh in the 3D viewport, but a node in the Shader Editor.

    Using a Mac?

    Save and undo use Cmd, and Alt is labelled Option. Most node and material shortcuts still use Ctrl.

    Blender 4.x changes

    The Principled BSDF was rebuilt in 4.0 (Coat, Sheen and Subsurface Weight), AgX became the default colour view in 4.0, and EEVEE was redesigned in 4.2. Older tutorials may look different.

        Step 4 · Build

        Wire the nodes

        Connect each node tree so the material works. Choose an output socket (right side of a node), then an input socket (left side) to connect them. Check settings such as Color Space too. Everything works with the keyboard: Tab to a socket and press Enter.

        Step 6 · Recognise

        Step 7 · Decide

        Scenario quiz: choose the best approach

        Step 9 · Make something

        Mini project: finish the coffee mug

        For tutors

        Teacher notes

        Purpose and audience

        Part 3 of an interactive Blender series for FE and HE students who have completed the Shortcut Trainer (Part 1) and Modifiers and Topology (Part 2). It covers materials with the Principled BSDF, the Shader Editor, procedural and image textures, colour space, surface detail, basic UV unwrapping, and the differences between EEVEE and Cycles.

        As in Parts 1 and 2, it moves from explanation (Learn) through experimentation (Material lab), building (Wire the nodes), diagnosis (Texture detective, Which material?) and decisions (Scenarios) to retrieval practice and a project in Blender.

        How this follows on from Parts 1 and 2

        Same look and features: progress indicator, Mac keys toggle, reference and cheat sheet, review deck, final challenge, summary and teacher notes all work as before.

        Prerequisite check: the warm-up recaps shortcuts from Part 1 (Z pie, Tab, 3, Ctrl+L, Ctrl+A) and shading ideas from Part 2 (Shade Auto Smooth, normals and Face Orientation, n-gon artefacts). Students scoring under 6/8 first time are pointed back to Part 2. Keep all three HTML files in the same folder so the links work.

        Progression: the mini project continues the Part 2 coffee mug. Solidify’s Material Offset links modifiers to material slots, and smooth shading and clean topology are shown to matter for glossy materials.

        Suggested session plan (two sessions of about 90 minutes)

        Session 1 — Materials and the Principled BSDF

        Starter (10 min): warm-up quiz.

        Intro (10 min): demonstrate Solid vs Material Preview vs Rendered, then render the same glass sphere in EEVEE and Cycles and compare time and quality.

        Main (35 min): Learn categories 1–6 with quick checks. Live demo: assigning a second material to faces.

        Practice (25 min): Material lab targets, then Which material? as a whole-class vote.

        Plenary (10 min): two or three scenarios in pairs.

        Session 2 — Nodes, textures and UVs

        Starter (5 min): review deck.

        Main 1 (25 min): Learn categories 7–10, Wire the nodes and Texture detective. Demonstrate Node Wrangler’s Ctrl+Shift+T with a free PBR texture set.

        Main 2 (45 min): coffee mug project, including both renders.

        Plenary (15 min): compare EEVEE and Cycles renders as a class; final challenge (or homework) and summary.

        Differentiation

        Support: core students can complete the project with Principled BSDF settings only (steps 1–5, 9 and 10), skipping textures and UVs. Use the lab presets, hint buttons, multiple-choice-only final challenge and printed cheat sheet.

        Stretch: the label and table texture steps, the extension tasks (decal with Alpha mixing, speckled procedural glaze with bump, steam), and a written EEVEE vs Cycles evaluation with render times.

        Accessibility: all activities work with a keyboard and screen reader. Node wiring uses buttons for sockets and lists every connection as text. The material lab describes what the material looks like in words.

        Suggested assessment evidence

        Learning summary (printed or copied), plus: an EEVEE render and a Cycles render of the mug with render times; a screenshot of the Shader Editor for one material; a screenshot of the mug’s UV layout with the test grid.

        Professional discussion: “Why is the glaze Metallic 0?”, “Which images are Non-Color, and why?”, “Which engine would you choose for a 250-frame turntable, and why?”

        Possible criteria: Pass — creates and assigns suitable materials, including two on one object. Merit — uses textures with correct colour space and an unwrapped UV map; renders in both engines. Distinction — produces convincing, reference-based materials, evaluates EEVEE vs Cycles with evidence, and diagnoses and fixes problems independently.

        Results are self-reported and stored only on the student’s device, so treat them as formative evidence.

        Practical and version notes

        A single HTML file with no external libraries: it works offline and can be shared through a VLE. Progress is stored in the browser’s local storage only.

        Content is based on Blender 4.x with the default keymap. Version notes: 4.0 rebuilt the Principled BSDF (Coat, Sheen, Subsurface Weight, Specular IOR Level) and made AgX the default view transform; 4.1 merged the Musgrave texture into Noise; 4.2 introduced the redesigned EEVEE (ray tracing, the material Render Method setting, bloom moved to the Compositor’s Glare node). Node Wrangler ships with Blender but must be enabled in Preferences › Add-ons.

        The material lab and preview spheres are hand-drawn approximations to build intuition about settings. They are not renders — encourage students to compare with Material Preview in Blender. For Cycles, check GPU support on classroom machines and set test render samples low (e.g. 64–128 with Denoise) to keep render times manageable.
