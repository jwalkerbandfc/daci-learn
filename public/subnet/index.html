    Step 1 · About 5 minutes

    Start here: picture a street

    Goal: understand what a network, a host and a subnet mask are, using a street of houses.

    The big idea

    Every device on a network needs an address, like a house needs an address. A computer's address is called an [[ip|IP address]]. It has two jobs in one: it says which street you live on and which house you are.

    The street = the [[network]]

    All the houses on one street share the same street name. Devices on one network share the same network part of the address.

    The house number = the [[host]]

    A host is any device: a laptop, a phone, a printer, a camera. Each gets its own house number on the street.

    The fence = the [[mask|subnet mask]]

    The mask is a rule that says where the street name stops and the house number starts.

    One street, 256 slots

    Take the network 192.168.10.0/24. The street is 192.168.10. The last number is the house. Two of the 256 slots are special. Hover, focus or tap the dotted words to learn more.

    .0  [[network address|street sign]] .1 first house.2.3 … .254 last house .255  [[broadcast|loudspeaker]]

    So this street has 256 slots but only 254 houses that devices can use. The sign and the loudspeaker are not homes. That is where the famous 2h − 2 rule comes from, and we will build it step by step.

    Step 2 · About 10 minutes

    Binary: eight light switches

    Goal: read an 8-bit [[binary]] number and turn it into a decimal number.

    Computers only count with ON and OFF

    A computer has no fingers. It has switches. A switch is OFF (0) or ON (1). One switch is a [[bit]]. Eight switches in a row are one [[octet]] (also called a byte).

    Each switch is worth a number. Starting from the right, each one is double the last: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128. When a switch is ON, you add its value. When it is OFF, you add nothing.

    Each bar is double the one before it. Hover a bar to see why.

    Biggest number in one octet: all eight switches ON = 128+64+32+16+8+4+2+1 = 255. Smallest: all OFF = 0. That is why each IP octet is between 0 and 255.

    Reading one octet: a guided look

    Press the tour button. A spotlight moves across the picture and tells you what to look at.

    What does 28 mean?

    You will see little raised numbers all the time in subnetting, such as 28 and 26. Say it as “two to the power of eight”.

    • The big number is the base. It is the number we multiply. In binary the base is always 2, because a switch has 2 states.
    • The small raised number is the [[exponent]], also called the power. It says how many times to write the base down.
    • Then multiply all of them together.

    Worked example: 28
    Step 1. Write 2 eight times: 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2.
    Step 2. Multiply from the left, one pair at a time: 2 × 2 = 4, then 4 × 2 = 8, then 8 × 2 = 16, then 16 × 2 = 32, then 32 × 2 = 64, then 64 × 2 = 128, then 128 × 2 = 256.
    Step 3. Spot the shortcut: every answer is double the one before.
    So 28 = 256.

    Why it matters. Eight switches can make 28 = 256 different patterns. They count from 0 to 255. Later, a subnet with h host bits has 2h addresses.

    Goal: turn a decimal number (0 to 255) into binary using the “does it fit?” method, and go back again.

    The “does it fit?” method

    Start with your number. Go along the place values from 128 down to 1. At each one ask: does this value fit into what I have left?

    • Yes: write 1 and take the value away.
    • No: write 0 and move on.

    Think of paying with coins that double in size. You always use the biggest coin that fits first.

    Step 5 · About 12 minutes

    Subnet masks and CIDR: the fence

    Goal: read a mask like 255.255.255.192 and /26, and say how many bits belong to the street and how many to the houses.

    The mask draws the fence

    A [[mask|subnet mask]] is 32 bits too. It is a row of 1s followed by a row of 0s. 1s = street (network). 0s = house number (host). The [[cidr|CIDR]] number, such as /26, simply counts the 1s.

    The fence can sit inside an octet. That is subnetting: we move the fence to the right to make smaller streets.

    Colour and letters: blue solid-underlined bits marked N are network. Orange dashed-underlined bits marked H are host. You never need colour to tell them apart.

    Step 6 · About 20 minutes

    CIDR: reading the slash

    Goal: look at any /prefix and say its mask, its size, its block size and which octet the fence sits in. Every challenge uses this, so take your time here.

    What CIDR means

    CIDR stands for Classless Inter-Domain Routing. Before 1993, networks came in three fixed sizes called classes. Imagine a shop that sells shoes in only three sizes. A company that needed 300 addresses had to take a Class B with 65,534. Most of it was wasted.

    Old classFirst octetFixed prefixUsable hosts
    A1 to 126/816,777,214
    B128 to 191/1665,534
    C192 to 223/24254

    CIDR removed the fixed sizes. The network part can now be any length, and the number after the slash says how long. The CCNA still expects you to know the classes, but real networks use CIDR.

    The one-line rule: the number after the slash counts the network bits (the 1s in the mask). A bigger slash number means more network bits, fewer host bits and a smaller subnet.

    Street picture

    The slash says how much of the address is the street name. /8 is a short street name with room for millions of houses. /30 is almost all street name, so only 2 houses.

    Just shorthand

    /26 is a quick way to write 255.255.255.192. Same fence, fewer characters.

    Why the exam cares

    Questions give you the mask or the slash. You must switch between them in seconds.

    Every extra slash halves the subnet

    Start with a /24 and add one to the slash each time. The block gets half as big every step. Hover a bar for details.

    The 32 bits, with the fence stops at /8, /16, /24 and /32

    Each number is a bit position. A bit is a network bit when the prefix is at least as big as its number. Thick edges mark the four octet boundaries.

    Four questions to ask about any slash

    1. Which octet holds the fence? Divide the prefix by 8, drop the remainder, add 1 (never more than 4). That is the first octet that is not 255 in the mask. Example: /21 → 21 ÷ 8 = 2, plus 1 = octet 3.
    2. What is the mask? Full octets are 255. In the fence octet, count the 1s (prefix minus 8 for every full octet) and add their values from the left: 128, 64, 32, 16, 8, 4, 2, 1. Octets after it are 0.
    3. How big is each subnet? Host bits h = 32 − prefix. Addresses = 2h. Usable = 2h − 2.
    4. Where do subnets start? Block size = 256 − the mask value in the fence octet. Subnets start at 0, then block size, then 2 × block size, and so on.

    CIDR reference table

    How to read a row. Each row is one prefix. Tap a prefix to get a plain-English explanation under the table.

    • Mask: the same fence in dotted decimal.
    • Host bits: 32 minus the prefix. These are the 0s.
    • Addresses: 2 to the power of the host bits. Every address in the subnet.
    • Usable hosts: addresses minus 2 (the network and broadcast addresses). A star means a special case.
    • Block size: the jump between one subnet and the next in the fence octet.
    • Fence octet: the octet where the 1s turn into 0s.

    * /31 gives 2 usable addresses on point-to-point links (RFC 3021). /32 is one single address.

    Four common CIDR mistakes

    • “/24 means 24 hosts.” No. It means 24 network bits. The hosts come from the other 8 bits: 254.
    • “Bigger slash means bigger network.” The opposite. /30 is tiny, /16 is large.
    • Forgetting the −2. Always remove the network and broadcast addresses (except /31 and /32).
    • Mixing up octets. /20 is a fence in octet 3, not octet 4. Use the divide-by-8 rule.

    Step 6 · About 15 minutes

    Host range calculator

    Goal: for any address and mask, find the network address, first host, last host, broadcast address and number of usable hosts, and know why.

    The five answers

    1. [[network address|Network address]]: all host bits 0. Found by an AND of the address and the mask.
    2. First host: network address + 1.
    3. Last host: broadcast − 1.
    4. [[broadcast|Broadcast address]]: all host bits 1.
    5. Usable hosts: 2h − 2, where h = number of [[host bits]].

    Worked example of 2h − 2 for a /26.
    Host bits h = 32 − 26 = 6.
    26 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 64 addresses.
    Take away the network address and the broadcast address: 64 − 2 = 62 usable hosts.

    [[and|AND]] rule: 1 AND 1 = 1, anything else is 0. Where the mask has 1s the address bit is kept. Where the mask has 0s the address bit is wiped to 0.

    Step 7 · About 12 minutes

    Slicing a network into equal subnets

    Goal: borrow host bits to make several equal subnets, and list every subnet range.

    Slicing a pizza

    A network is a whole pizza. Cutting it into equal slices is fixed-length subnetting. Each time you borrow one host bit for the network part, you double the number of slices and halve their size.

    • Borrow n bits → 2n subnets.
    • Host bits left h = 32 − new prefix → 2h − 2 hosts each.

    Cut the pizza in half, again and again

    Picture 192.168.1.0/24 as a pizza made of 256 address slices. Every time you borrow one more host bit, every slice is cut in half. Drag the slider.

    Scenario: 72, 15, 8 and 3 devices

    Goal: use [[vlsm|VLSM]] to give each team a right-sized subnet from one network, with no overlaps.

    Sharing a chocolate bar

    You have one bar of 256 squares: 192.168.10.0/24. Four teams need different amounts. Equal slices would waste squares. With VLSM we break off big pieces first, then smaller ones, so nothing overlaps.

    1. List the needs from largest to smallest.
    2. For each, add 2 (sign and loudspeaker). Find the smallest power of 2 that fits. That is the block size.
    3. Count the host bits → prefix = 32 − host bits.
    4. Place each block, one after the other, starting where the last one ended.

    Step 10 · At your own pace

    VLSM practice lab

    Goal: complete a full CCNA subnet table for a VLSM design: number of hosts, network address, first and last host, broadcast address, and subnet mask with CIDR.

    The VLSM method in five moves

    1. List the needs, largest first. Big blocks must start on big boundaries, so they go in before the small ones.
    2. Number of hosts. Add 2 to the need (and 1 more if the router interface takes an address). Jump up to the next power of 2. That is the block size. Usable hosts = block size − 2.
    3. CIDR and mask. The power of 2 is your host bits. Prefix = 32 − host bits. Then write the mask.
    4. Network address. The first block starts at the base network. Every other block starts at the previous broadcast + 1, which must be a multiple of its own block size.
    5. First, last and broadcast. First host = network + 1. Broadcast = network + block size − 1. Last host = broadcast − 1. The next free address is the broadcast + 1.

    Step 10 · Revision

    CCNA exam toolkit

    Goal: know the exam objective, the speed method, the common traps, and keep a cheat sheet.

    Exam objective

    This guide covers the IPv4 addressing and subnetting skills in the Cisco CCNA 200-301 blueprint, objective 1.6 “Configure and verify IPv4 addressing and subnetting”, plus the private addressing ideas in the same topic area. IPv6 (objective 1.8 and related) is not covered here. Blueprints change, so always check the current exam topics on Cisco’s website. This is an independent study aid, not an official Cisco resource.

    Five-step speed method

    1. Mask: convert /prefix to dotted decimal.
    2. Interesting octet: the one that is not 0 or 255 (or the last one).
    3. Block size: 256 − mask octet.
    4. Network: largest multiple of the block size that is ≤ your octet.
    5. Broadcast: next network − 1. Hosts sit in between.

    Practise this until it takes under 30 seconds. The exam gives about 1 to 2 minutes per question.

    Common exam traps

    • Usable vs total. A /26 has 64 addresses but 62 usable hosts.
    • /31 and /32. /31 gives 2 addresses and is valid on point-to-point links (RFC 3021). /32 is a single host route. The 2h−2 rule does not apply to them.
    • Count every interface. A router interface (the [[default gateway]]) uses an address too. 8 devices + gateway = 9 hosts.
    • Largest first in VLSM, and each block starts on a multiple of its own size.
    • Network and broadcast cannot be assigned to hosts on normal subnets.
    • Private ranges: 172.16.0.0 to 172.31.255.255 only. 172.32.x.x is public.
    • [[wildcard|Wildcard mask]] (used in ACLs and OSPF network statements) = 255.255.255.255 minus the subnet mask. Example: /26 mask 255.255.255.192 gives wildcard 0.0.0.63.
